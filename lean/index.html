Formalization

Lean

Lean is a proof assistant: a language in which mathematical definitions, theorems, and proofs are written precisely enough that a computer can check every step.

What is Lean?

Lean is an interactive theorem prover. In ordinary mathematics a proof is a piece of prose that a reader follows and, if convinced, accepts. In Lean a proof is a formal object. Every definition is spelled out, every theorem is a precise statement in a typed logic, and every proof is a sequence of steps that a small, trusted kernel verifies. If Lean accepts the proof, it is correct, all the way down to the axioms.

Formalizing mathematics in Lean means translating a piece of mathematics into this language. One first writes down the objects involved (a modular form, an L-function, a Hecke operator) as definitions, then states the theorem in terms of those definitions, and finally builds the proof out of previously formalized results and tactics, which are small automated procedures that carry out routine steps. The process is slower than writing a proof on paper, but at the end there is no ambiguity about what was proved or whether the proof is complete.

Why I care

Correctness. My research is in analytic number theory, and the arguments in my field are long and delicate. Constants, error terms, and edge cases are easy to get wrong, and the usual safeguards are careful refereeing and the passage of time. A formalized proof removes the doubt. It also tends to reveal exactly where the real content of an argument lives, which is useful in its own right.

Mathematics that machines can build on. Large language models are becoming part of how mathematics gets done, but they hallucinate: they will state a theorem slightly wrong, or cite a result that does not quite exist. If a result is formalized in Lean, an AI system can use it with no risk of misremembering it, and anything it proves from it can be checked by Lean. Formalizing our work makes it possible for these tools to make new discoveries that are formally known to be true, rather than merely plausible.

What I am working on

I am currently formalizing all of my papers in Lean. For each paper the plan is to formalize the definitions and theorem statements first, then the proofs.

Alongside this I am making videos, posted to my YouTube channel Screech Math, that place the natural language statement of each theorem next to its Lean formalization and walk through them together. A formal proof is only as good as the statement it proves, and the point of the videos is to make sure that the translation from the paper to Lean is faithful, in a way that anyone can check.
